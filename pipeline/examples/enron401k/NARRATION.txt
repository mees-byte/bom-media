[HOOK]
Your boss tells you the company stock is a bargain. So you keep your retirement in it. Then your 401(k) gets locked. And within weeks, your life savings are almost gone. That happened to thousands of workers at one of the biggest companies in America. And the trap they fell into? You can still fall into it today.

[CH1 — THE DREAM COMPANY]
Enron. Houston, Texas. In 2001 it ranked seventh on the Fortune 500. Its stock hit about ninety dollars in August 2000. Working there felt like winning the lottery.
And the 401(k) made it even better. When workers saved, Enron matched them. But the match was paid in Enron stock. And workers could not sell that match until they turned fifty.
Many also bought more Enron stock with their own money. By 2001, more than sixty percent of the money in the 401(k) plan was in one stock. Enron.

[CH2 — "AN INCREDIBLE BARGAIN"]
In August 2001, the CEO, Jeff Skilling, quit after just six months on the job. The stock kept sliding.
On September 26th, chairman Ken Lay held an online chat with employees. He told them the company was fundamentally sound. He called the stock, then around twenty-five dollars, an incredible bargain.
Here's what workers didn't see. A vice president had already warned Lay in writing about the accounting. And according to a shareholder lawsuit, top Enron insiders had sold about a billion dollars of stock over the previous few years.

[CH3 — THE LOCKDOWN]
On October 16th, Enron reported a six hundred eighteen million dollar loss. In the days that followed, the stock fell hard.
And days later, the 401(k) plan was frozen. Enron said it was a long-planned switch to a new plan administrator. Workers could not sell. Not a single share.
By Enron's own account, the freeze ran from late October to mid-November. The stock went from thirteen eighty-one to nine ninety-eight while their hands were tied. Workers say it started even earlier.
On December 2nd, 2001, Enron filed for bankruptcy. By then, the stock was below one dollar.
One worker's 401(k) had been worth about four hundred seventy thousand dollars. It dropped to about forty thousand. Another retiree says he lost one point three million.
In total, workers say they lost more than a billion dollars in their retirement plans.

[CH4 — WHAT CHANGED]
Workers sued. Years later, settlements paid back part of it. An eighty-five million dollar insurance deal. Thirty-seven and a half million from the plan's trustee. A fraction of what was lost.
Congress changed the rules too. Since 2003, your plan generally has to warn you at least thirty days before a 401(k) blackout. And since 2007, if your 401(k) holds your employer's publicly traded stock, you have the right to sell it. Including the company match, after three years on the job.

[CH5 — YOUR 401(k) TODAY]
But here's the part nobody tells you. The rules can warn you. They can't force you to sell.
Some workers, especially at big public companies, still hold a big piece of their retirement in their own employer's stock. Same company that pays your salary. Same company your whole future depends on.
So open your statement. See how much is in one company. And if you get a blackout notice, read it.
This is not financial advice. Just the lesson Enron workers paid for with their retirement.
